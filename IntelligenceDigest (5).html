Energy has stopped being a market and started being a set of ownership claims

Governments are no longer buying barrels and cargoes. They are buying the reserves, the shipping lanes and the fields themselves. Underneath that, a quieter story: the world keeps announcing capacity it cannot physically switch on.

01

Countries have started buying oil fields instead of oil

The big change is not the price of energy. It is who owns the thing that makes it.

For about 50 years, rich countries handled energy risk by buying the product. If oil got scarce you paid more for a cargo. You did not own the ground. That has now changed in the open.

The United States has announced a deal covering 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil across 17 undeveloped fields. A new private company gets rights for 100 years, the American side takes 55% of the output plus an ownership stake and the right to buy oil at cost, about 100 billion dollars goes into Venezuela's oil industry, and Venezuela expects over 209 billion dollars in tax. A proven reserve is oil known to be in the ground that can be pulled out at today's prices — a claim on future barrels, not cash. The world holds about 1.57 trillion. America has about 46 billion of its own, so this takes it to roughly 111 billion, or 7.1% of everything proven on earth.

100 yrsLength of the rights
55%Share of output to the American side
7.1%Share of world proven reserves controlled

At 83 dollars a barrel those reserves look worth 5.4 trillion dollars gross. But Venezuelan oil is thick and full of sulphur, so it costs more to refine and sells below the headline price. After discounts and drilling costs, a realistic 25 to 35 dollars a barrel gives about 1.25 trillion, spread over the life of the fields. Both numbers are honest, which is why this is either the biggest energy deal in history or a claim needing decades of money to turn real. Venezuela's equipment is in poor shape, and a promise about output in 2040 does nothing about a shortage now.

The same instinct is everywhere. Prize courts are being revived — an old wartime system, barely used since the age of sail, letting a state seize an enemy's cargo ship and keep what is on it — this time aimed at Iranian oil. The Falklands are leverage to push Britain's defence spending up. America is pushing into Africa against China. The emergency oil store is to be filled with Venezuelan crude. None of that is trading; each is about owning a physical thing. So a commodity business is now worth partly whichever government it sits under, and country risk has stopped being a footnote.

02

The things that cannot be conjured

Announced capacity and working capacity have drifted apart in 3 industries at once.

The bottleneck has moved. It is no longer money, and often not even chips. It is equipment, and the factories that make it.

Roughly 15 gigawatts of artificial-intelligence computing built in 2027 will not be switched on in 2027 — not for want of power stations, but for want of transformers, wiring, liquid cooling, chillers and networking. 1 gigawatt is about 1 large nuclear reactor, or power for 750,000 homes, so 15 gigawatts is a mid-sized country's demand, paid for and idle. Gigawatts were the right measure for the cloud era; for artificial intelligence the contents matter more than the container. If the shell gets built and cannot be filled, the money is inside — transformers, cooling, switchgear — not in land with a grid connection, and the announced pipeline of computing power overstates what will really run.

India offered subsidies for 50 gigawatt-hours of advanced battery cell factories. About 1.4 gigawatt-hours is running, from 1 company. Official reviews blame supply problems, strict local-content rules, a 2-year deadline nobody could meet, and visa delays for the Chinese engineers needed to install the machines. 1 award of 20 gigawatt-hours was handed back and no subsidy money has been paid out. The remaining 10 gigawatt-hours has been re-offered for grid storage, needing 25% Indian value within 2 years and 40% within 5.

Assembly — putting bought-in cells into packs and containers — added 6.35 gigawatt-hours in 1 month, over 4 times what the cell scheme managed in years. India is good at the last step and has barely started the first, so most of the value in every battery project leaves the country.

Two more of the same shape. 45 Indian coal plants are running below 25% of the coal stock they should hold, with monsoon hitting mining and heat lifting demand. And the biggest oil tankers, carrying about 2 million barrels each, now earn around 650,000 dollars a day — what happens when the ships rather than the oil are scarce, as routes lengthen and insurance jumps. In all 4 cases there is plenty of the headline resource and a missing physical link that takes years to build. Whoever owns that link gets paid first, and they are dull businesses: transformer makers, cooling suppliers, cell manufacturers, tanker owners.

03

Money and rates

Borrowing costs are back at 2008 levels while the money supply sets a record every month.

America's central bank chief said price stability is now the main focus and inflation is still too high. Markets read that as a firm no to rate cuts, and moved at once.

What moved, and what each number means
MeasureLevelWhat it tells you
30-year bond yieldnear 5.21%What America pays to borrow for 30 years. Back to 2008 levels. Sets the floor for mortgages and company debt everywhere.
5-year bond yield4.48%Highest since early 2025. The medium-term view of where rates settle.
Goldbelow 4,500 dollars/ozFell on the news. Gold pays no interest, so higher rates make holding it costlier.
Money supply23.22 trillion dollarsA record, up 102.8 billion in the latest month — the 27th monthly rise in a row.

That last figure counts what people and firms can actually spend — cash, current accounts, savings, small deposits. It has grown 862.7 billion dollars this year and sits 1.43 trillion above its early-2022 peak, against normal growth of about 6.3% a year. So the bank is talking tight and acting loose, which gives exactly this result: borrowing is dear, yet hard assets keep climbing.

Over 10 years American shares beat American government bonds by 15 percentage points a year, the widest gap since the late 1950s, counting dividends and interest on both sides. A gap that big for that long is how an asset becomes universally disliked, and dislike is usually where the next decade's returns start — though the reason bonds lost is the reason they may keep losing: a government that keeps borrowing.

Currencies lose almost everything quietly. 1 pound from 1976 needs to be 9.34 pounds today, so 90% is gone; 1 rupee needs to be 31 rupees, so 97% is gone. Gold is still only 2.7% of all financial assets in the world while debt keeps rising and nearly every big economy runs a deficit — governments do not go bankrupt, they devalue, and inflation is how the bill gets paid. But those numbers are about cash alone: over the same 50 years, money in shares or even earning interest grew a great deal.

One warning worth filing. The American Treasury Secretary said a disorderly yen could push American interest rates up. Japanese institutions are among the largest foreign owners of American government debt; if the yen moves violently they sell those bonds and bring money home, and forced selling pushes yields up. A currency problem 10,000 kilometres away becomes an American mortgage problem.

04

Food and fuel

Farm prices did something seen 5 times in 125 years, and Asian gas more than doubled.

A broad basket of farm commodities — wheat, corn, soybeans, sugar, coffee, cotton, cocoa, livestock — rose for 11 sessions in a row, gaining 13.4% to a 3-year high. In 125 years of records only 5 stretches ran longer. The last was in the 1930s.

Wheat is at the centre, at 7.84 dollars a bushel (about 27 kilograms), up 22.9% in the month and about 50% this year. Russia and Ukraine ship more than 25% of the world's exported wheat, and ports along both Black Sea coasts are disrupted. Ukraine has cut its expected farm exports for the coming season to 29.6 million tonnes from 64.4 million.

The Strait of Hormuz is a sea passage between Iran and Oman, only a couple of miles wide where ships pass. About 20% of the world's shipped natural gas goes through it, most from a single Qatari terminal, and there is no other route — the gas is on ships, and the ships have one door.

MMBtu is a fixed-size bucket of gas, used so prices in different countries can be compared. Gas inside America got cheaper while Asia and Europe roughly doubled — a big, lasting cost advantage for anything power-hungry built on American soil, and a matching penalty for the same plant in India, Japan, Korea or Germany. Over a few years that decides where chemical plants, fertiliser plants, smelters and data centres get built.

In India, compressed natural gas around Delhi rose 3.89 rupees a kilogram, the 5th rise this year after 4 earlier ones totalling 6 rupees. Delhi is now 86.98 rupees a kilogram, Noida and Ghaziabad 95.59, Meerut 95.47, Gurugram 92.01. Petrol is up about 7.5 rupees a litre since the conflict began, and blending ethanol into petrol cuts fuel efficiency by 2% to 6%, so the real cost per kilometre rose more than the pump price shows. Before this rise Delhi's price had gone up only 7.8% while the international benchmark it buys against rose 113% — that gap is the state absorbing the shock, and it comes back later as thinner distributor margins, a subsidy bill, or more catch-up rises.

Copper is the quiet one. Supply is peaking, demand is rising and the money supply keeps growing, so copper measured against the amount of money is climbing. Electrifying everything needs copper, and a large new mine takes about a decade from discovery to first metal.

New shares compete for the same savings as existing ones, so when paper grows faster than the money chasing it the market turns selective, and that 11.5% average gain is the first thing to fall.

The 3-wheeler number is the useful one. 3-wheelers are 61% electric and 2-wheelers 8.3%, with the same country, subsidy, charging and electricity price. The only difference is daily distance. A 3-wheeler covering 100 kilometres a day pays back the higher price in months at 8 times lower running cost; a 2-wheeler covering 20 takes years. So adoption converts categories in order of kilometres driven, which means fleets, delivery riders and taxis go long before private families.

Owning the vehicle maker is the hard way to play it. 1 of the 2 listed electric 2-wheeler companies is worth about 64,000 crore rupees against about 18,000 crore for the other, a gap of 3.5 times, while selling only about 2.3 times as many vehicles, with the smaller one recovering share. A motorcycle manufacturer is raising its stake in the larger to 32.8% from 29.88%, spending 1,758 crore rupees in the open market plus 960 crore in convertible instruments. The smaller has launched a scooter at 79,999 rupees for 3.1 kilowatt-hours and 99,999 for 5.1, using cells from its own factory; the larger has a rival model under 100,000 rupees with a 2,000 crore plant starting production. So the middle of this chain is a price war where gross profit has only just reached parity with petrol, while the parts maker earns 2 to 3 times the content whichever badge wins and the cell maker owns the largest cost in the product against almost no domestic competition.

Grid battery storage shows the same split between now and later. Tendering fell 26% to 8.4 gigawatts and awards fell 65%, from 6.9 gigawatts to 2.4 — yet commissioning accelerated, with 8,236 megawatt-hours going live over 4 months plus 250 megawatts of pumped hydro. Those are 18 to 24 months apart: what switches on now was awarded 2 years ago, and what is awarded now switches on 2 years from here. So a 65% collapse in awards is a commissioning cliff around 2028, arriving just as all the new solar needs somewhere to go after sunset.

One export business is quietly compounding through all of it. Tyre exports rose 16% to about 7,700 crore rupees in the June quarter, on a record 27,312 crore for the full previous year. Passenger car tyres led at 21% growth, Europe rose 25% to 3,003 crore or about 40% of the total, and the United States is the largest single market at 16%. Indian tyres now reach over 170 countries, despite the high input and freight costs that were meant to be the problem.

How India is spending, and who is collecting

A festival moved online in one step, milk got 9.7% dearer, and delivery workers started leaving.

Raksha Bandhan is a sibling festival where a decorated thread is tied on the wrist, traditionally bought in a local market. That habit has broken.

Men's kurtas rose 87% as a last-minute buy, and the whole gifting segment roughly tripled. Every platform reports the same 2 things: premium items growing faster than standard, and delivery windows collapsing to minutes. When the decision is made 5 minutes before the gift is given there is no time to compare prices, so the buyer takes what is shown first and pays more for it. Quick commerce is not winning on price — it has moved the purchase to a moment when price stops mattering.

The people delivering it are leaving. The pool of workers available for delivery jobs shrank 5% to 10% over 4 months. They are not unemployed — they are moving to manufacturing, warehousing, retail and sales, because petrol is up about 7.5 rupees a litre and in most arrangements the rider pays that themselves.

Warehouse work draws 350% more candidates per opening than delivery — workers choosing a fixed wage over a headline figure they must fund with their own fuel. The chain is tight: the fuel shock in section 04 raises the rider's cost, which raises the wage a platform must offer, while handing over an electric vehicle becomes the cheapest pay rise available, because running cost is 8 times lower. At home, wholesale milk in Mumbai goes from 93 to 102 rupees a litre, fixed for 6 months from the start of September. That is the grain and fodder cost from section 04 arriving directly, and unlike fuel there is no state distributor to cushion it. Elsewhere, the head of a large American discount retailer said sales grew across every income group, skewed towards middle and higher income households — when food and fuel rise, wealthier households do not stop spending, they change where. So an inflation like this hurts mid-priced brands more than either the value end or the premium end.

The 50 largest companies compounded at 7.7% a year over 5 years, which after Indian inflation is close to nothing. Mid-sized companies did more than twice as well and beat small companies over both 5 and 10 years — the opposite of the usual assumption. They are big enough to have real businesses and small enough to still grow, while the largest are mostly banks, oil and software, exactly the sectors falling. Size and fame have been a drag here, not a protection.

India's largest private bank is changing leader. The managing director will not seek reappointment and steps down when his term ends on 26 October. Over his near 6-year tenure the price-to-book multiple fell from about 4 times to about 1.7. Book value is roughly the bank's assets minus its debts, so 4 times meant investors paid 4 rupees for every 1 rupee of net assets, expecting unusually high earnings on them for a long time. 1.7 means they no longer do — the assets barely changed, the belief about them did. A very large merger during that period changed the balance sheet and created its own problems, so blaming 1 person is unfair.

A large Chinese electric vehicle maker reported quarterly net profit of 8.2 billion yuan, about 1.22 billion dollars, up 30% — its first rise in over a year, but below the 48% expected. Revenue fell 3.2% to 194.6 billion yuan, a 4th straight decline, while gross margin improved to 18.85% from 18.01% on overseas sales. Profit up, revenue down, margin up on exports means fewer cars sold at home and more abroad at better prices. That is a healthier mix, not a recovery, and modelling the business on domestic volume is modelling the wrong thing.

Two warnings about believing management. One investor worked through a company's guidance in public — 10 systems in September, 25 in November, about 40 a month from January, supporting a 400 crore rupee revenue target — and concluded not that it was wrong but that guidance is an input, not a fact. A sharper version: compare aggressive guidance against how soon the company then raises money from institutions, and a number of decade-long themes resolve themselves within a week.

What one country is doing that another is not

A 40-year-old currency lesson being used early, and a technology handover with no precedent.

A common worry is that if China's currency were forced sharply higher, its car exports would collapse. The last time this happened tells a different story. In 1985 the major economies agreed to push the Japanese yen up sharply — the Plaza Accord, named after the New York hotel where it was signed. Japanese-branded cars held about 26% to 29% of the world market before it. 30 years later they held about 27% to 31%. The share did not fall; the cars were simply built inside the destination countries instead.

China is doing that earlier than Japan did, with car and battery plants already going up in Thailand, Indonesia, Spain, Brazil and Hungary. The shape is different too. At its peak Japan built about 6 million vehicles for export against 4 million for home, and Korea is similar at 2.4 million against 1.4 million. China's main market is China; exports are secondary. A currency revaluation hurts an exporter that depends on exports, and barely touches a maker whose home market is the largest in the world and whose overseas factories are already built. For India that is uncomfortable: tariffs and currency moves will not keep Chinese vehicles out, because the next wave arrives as a factory in a neighbouring country, inside an existing trade agreement.

Chinese technology is now competing on price rather than permission. Domestic equipment for advanced 3-dimensional chip stacking has been validated and put into production, giving Chinese chipmakers an alternative to the single European supplier that dominates that market. Chinese language models are in a price war among themselves, with the order inverted — the one assumed cheapest is now dearest, and a rival is both cheapest and most capable — and all of them run on domestic chips at large scale. A major Chinese cloud provider has opened its first South American data centre in Brazil. Validated is the important word: equipment enters a chip line when it works and yields, not on patriotism, and once a domestic alternative works the leverage of export restrictions decays permanently. Meanwhile global asset managers are reported to be pulling money out of China, so the financial and industrial pictures point opposite ways.

Set that launcher handover against the battery-cell failure in section 02 and a pattern shows. Those are opposite positions in one industry: memory is where demand runs ahead of what can be built, while the mature end has finished building and turns far more revenue into cash. Confusing the two is how people get the cycle wrong.

10

Arguments worth keeping open

4 real disagreements, each with a way to settle it.

Where credible people contradict each other, the disagreement is the information. Averaging 2 opposing views gives something nobody believes and nothing that can be tested.

Is the spending on artificial intelligence a bubble?

Yes, says one side: this spending cycle is larger than any in history, every previous mania ended in a bubble that popped, and this time will not be different. American public opinion is more sceptical than excited about the technology, the predicted wave of job losses has not arrived, and 79% of the whole market's gain this earnings season came from 1 sector, with 2 companies accounting for 1.42 trillion dollars of it while the other 71 companies in that sector lost 22.3 billion between them.

No, say the people supplying the physical inputs. The memory manufacturer expects no downturn through 2030 and the power chip company has finished investing. And 15 gigawatts of computing built in 2027 cannot be switched on that year for want of transformers, cooling and networking. A bubble is capacity nobody wants; this is capacity people want and cannot get.

To tell which, watch whether the shortage moves or vanishes. In a real physical shortage the bottleneck migrates — chips to power, power to transformers, transformers to cooling — while lead times stay long throughout. In a bubble, lead times collapse everywhere at once as orders are cancelled. The clearest single signal is the order book at transformer and switchgear makers, furthest from the excitement and closest to the constraint.

Which of India's 2 listed electric 2-wheeler makers is mispriced?

The larger is worth about 64,000 crore rupees against about 18,000 crore for its rival, a gap of 3.5 times, while selling only about 2.3 times as many vehicles, with the rival recovering share. The rival also owns its own cell factory and a battery storage business covering homes, businesses and the grid. On that reading, one is priced for perfection and the other for permanent failure.

Against that, the larger has the product and the sponsor. Its motorcycle-manufacturing backer is raising its stake to 32.8% from 29.88%, spending 1,758 crore rupees in the open market plus 960 crore in convertible instruments, and buying in the open market is a costlier signal than a private placement. Its new scooter is under 100,000 rupees with a 2,000 crore plant starting production and a software subscription layer behind it.

Do not settle it with company sales claims — use the government registration database, which counts vehicles actually registered rather than sent to dealers, since that gap has embarrassed this sector before. Then track gross profit per vehicle each quarter, and cell capacity actually running rather than announced. With only 1.4 gigawatt-hours live nationally against 50 promised, a company that genuinely makes cells owns something almost nobody else does.

Is the bank leadership change a repair or an admission?

One view is that the board failed. Many said there would never be a second bank like this one, and that may have held only while the founding chief executive was in the seat; since then internal problems have cost both the chair and the managing director in quick succession.

The other is that this is the reset. A rival private bank changed leader in 2018 and asset quality, profitability and execution all improved, with the market following. At 1.7 times book, expectations are already low enough for a new chief executive to reset them.

The parallel only holds if the problem is the same kind. The 2018 case was a governance and credit crisis, which a new leader can fix by changing behaviour. This bank's difficulty came from absorbing a very large merger that permanently changed its funding mix — a lending book arrived without the cheap deposits to fund it, and no chief executive can undo that by deciding to. So watch the share of deposits sitting in low-interest current and savings accounts, and the cost of deposits. If those improve over 4 to 6 quarters the reset is real; if not, the derating was correct.

Is India's battery storage industry booming or about to stall?

Booming, on the building data: 8,236 megawatt-hours went live over 4 months plus 250 megawatts of pumped hydro, and 6.35 gigawatt-hours of assembly capacity opened in a single month. Stalling, on the order data: awards collapsed 65% from 6.9 gigawatts to 2.4, tendering fell 26%, and cell manufacturing is still minimal so the industry runs on imported cells.

Both are true, 18 to 24 months apart. The test is public: watch the award figure over the next 2 or 3 quarters. If it recovers towards 6.9 gigawatts this was a pause caused by tender terms. If it stays near 2.4, the cliff lands around 2028, exactly when the new solar needs somewhere to go after sunset.

Words explained

Every term used above, in plain language.

Proven reserveOil known to be in the ground that can be pulled out at today's prices. A claim on future barrels, not cash.
Heavy, sour crudeThick oil with a lot of sulphur. Costlier to refine, so it sells below the headline oil price.
Strategic petroleum reserveAn emergency store of crude a government keeps underground for a supply crisis.
Prize courtAn old legal body deciding whether a captured enemy ship and its cargo belong to the captor.
Strait of HormuzA sea passage between Iran and Oman, a couple of miles wide. About 20% of shipped gas passes through, with no other route.
VLCCVery large crude carrier — the biggest oil tanker, holding about 2 million barrels.
MMBtuA fixed-size bucket of gas energy, used so prices in different countries can be compared.
JKM, TTF, Henry HubThe spot gas price benchmarks for East Asia, Europe and the United States.
Spot marketBuying for immediate delivery at today's price, not under a long-term contract.
BushelA grain measure. A bushel of wheat is about 27 kilograms.
Money supply (M2)Money that can actually be spent: cash, current accounts, savings and small deposits.
Bond yieldThe yearly return on a government loan. When it rises, the government pays more to borrow, and so does everyone else.
Price-to-bookShare price divided by net assets per share. Above 1 means investors expect better than ordinary returns on those assets.
Compound annual growthThe steady yearly rate that would produce the total change seen, smoothing good and bad years into 1 number.
Basis pointOne hundredth of a percentage point. 170 basis points is 1.7 percentage points.
Gross profitRevenue minus the direct cost of making the product. It shows whether the product itself makes money.
PenetrationThe share of sales in a category taken by one type of product. 6.2% means 6.2 of every 100 new cars were electric.
LFPLithium iron phosphate — a cheaper, longer-lasting battery chemistry that stores slightly less energy for its weight.
Gigawatt and gigawatt-hourA gigawatt is a rate of power, about 1 large nuclear reactor. A gigawatt-hour is that rate for 1 hour, so an amount of stored energy.
Tendering, awarding, commissioningThree stages 18 to 24 months apart: putting a project out to bid, giving it to a winner, and switching it on.
Domestic value additionThe share of a product's value that must be made inside the country to qualify for a subsidy.
Input tax creditLets a business deduct tax already paid on its purchases, so a product is not taxed at every stage.
Open offerBuying control of a listed Indian company forces you to offer to buy a further slice from ordinary shareholders.
Reverse mergerAn unlisted company merges into a listed one and becomes publicly traded without its own share sale.
Offer for saleThe part of a share sale where existing owners sell their own shares, so the company gets none of that money.
Foreign portfolio investorAn overseas investor buying listed shares and bonds rather than building a factory. This money can leave quickly.
Foreign exchange reservesForeign currency and gold a central bank holds, used to pay for imports and defend the currency.
Plaza AccordA 1985 agreement among major economies to push the Japanese yen sharply higher against the dollar.
Ethanol blendingMixing crop alcohol into petrol. It cuts oil imports but also cuts fuel efficiency by 2% to 6%.
Quick commerceDelivery of everyday goods within minutes from small local warehouses.
Gig workPaid per task rather than per hour, usually with the worker covering fuel and vehicle costs.
Cheap depositsThe share of a bank's deposits in low-interest everyday accounts — the main driver of its funding cost.
Depositary receiptA certificate on a foreign exchange representing shares in a company listed elsewhere.
Crore and lakhIndian number units. 1 lakh is 100,000, 1 crore is 10 million, 1 lakh crore is 1 trillion.
